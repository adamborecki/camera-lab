Camera Simulator Webapp — Project Handoff / Design Brief

I’m building an interactive camera simulator webapp for teaching video recording, primarily for university students learning basic AV / concert recording skills.

This should not just be a toy or a collection of disconnected sliders. The goal is to make something that feels like an interactive science-museum exhibit or something like Ableton’s “Learning Synths”: visually immediate, intuitive, exploratory, but also grounded enough in real camera behavior that students build useful instincts.

The app will likely live on GitHub Pages and should work well in a browser on laptops and modern phones.

I am not trying to finalize every technical decision myself. I want the coding agent to take the design goals below, ask me a few useful follow-up questions, then help architect and build it.

⸻

Overall Goal

The webapp should teach students how video camera settings affect an image.

The key idea is that students should be able to manipulate camera settings and immediately see a plausible visual result.

I especially want them to develop intuition about:

* Aperture
* Shutter speed
* ISO / gain
* Frame rate
* White balance
* ND filters
* Focus
* Depth of field
* Motion blur
* Exposure
* Overexposure / clipping
* Underexposure
* Noise

This is explicitly a video camera simulator, not primarily a still-photography simulator.

Still-camera concepts overlap, obviously, but the pedagogy should revolve around video behavior.

For example, shutter speed should be understood relative to frame rate, and concepts like a 180-degree shutter / approximately 1/(2 × frame rate) should be teachable.

⸻

Core Design Philosophy

The simulator should be:

* Interactive
* Visually immediate
* Playful
* Accurate enough to teach useful real-world instincts
* Modular
* Easy to expand
* Friendly on phones and computers
* Capable of showing both good and bad camera settings
* Able to support guided teaching and independent challenges

I do not want a giant intimidating interface with every possible control and visualization visible at once.

Instead, I want a modular system where different activities or “stations” can expose only the controls and visualizations relevant to the concept being taught.

For example:

* One activity might only expose aperture.
* Another might expose aperture + ISO.
* Another might lock frame rate and teach shutter speed.
* Another might introduce white balance.
* Another might become a full-camera challenge.

The underlying simulation can be sophisticated, but the student-facing activity can remain simple.

⸻

Visual Scene Approach

The current design direction is:

Use several underlying visual scenes/assets, combined with a rendering/simulation layer, rather than trying to build a full 3D world.

That seems like the best balance between:

* realism,
* educational usefulness,
* performance,
* implementation complexity,
* and expandability.

Possible scenes could eventually include things like:

* Outdoor daylight
* Indoor room
* Concert stage
* Low-light performance
* Mixed lighting
* Moving subject
* Backlit subject
* High-contrast scene
* Skin tones / person
* Colorful stage lighting

The scene system should be modular enough that additional scenes can be added later.

I am open to the coding agent proposing the best method for this.

⸻

MVP Scope

I want the MVP to include a fairly complete camera-control set rather than only exposure.

The MVP should ideally include:

Exposure-related controls

* Aperture
* Shutter speed
* ISO / gain
* Frame rate
* ND

Color-related controls

* White balance
* Ideally color temperature in Kelvin
* Potentially tint later, if useful

Image-character controls

* Focus
* Depth of field
* Motion blur
* ISO noise

The implementation does not have to simulate optical physics perfectly, but these effects should be meaningful and visually educational.

⸻

Camera Physics / Accuracy

I would like the simulation to be more realistic rather than merely arbitrary, as long as that is practical in a browser.

I understand that real camera systems are complicated and that an RGB image is not equivalent to actual light hitting a sensor.

I do not necessarily need a full spectral renderer.

However, I would like the math and behavior to be based on real camera relationships whenever possible.

For example:

Aperture

Exposure contribution should reflect the actual relationship:

Light reaching the sensor is proportional to approximately:

1 / f-number²

Therefore:

* f/2.8 → f/4 is approximately -1 stop
* f/4 → f/2.8 is approximately +1 stop

The simulator should use actual stop relationships rather than arbitrary brightness multipliers.

Aperture should also affect depth of field.

⸻

Shutter Speed

Exposure should scale with shutter duration.

For example:

1/30 second receives approximately twice as much light as 1/60 second.

That is +1 stop.

Shutter should also affect motion blur.

Because this is a video simulator, shutter speed should interact meaningfully with frame rate.

⸻

Frame Rate

Frame rate should be treated as a real video parameter, not just a label.

Examples:

* 24 fps
* 25 fps
* 30 fps
* 50 fps
* 60 fps

The app should be able to teach the idea that appropriate shutter speed depends partly on frame rate.

For example:

30 fps → approximately 1/60 sec for a conventional 180-degree shutter look.

Students should also be able to intentionally break that relationship.

For example:

30 fps + 1/15 shutter

or

30 fps + 1/1000 shutter

and visually see why those choices behave differently.

⸻

ISO / Gain

ISO should brighten the resulting image through gain rather than pretending that more photons were captured.

This distinction matters conceptually.

Higher ISO should therefore:

* make the image appear brighter
* increase visible noise / image degradation

Lower ISO should:

* produce cleaner results
* require more actual exposure from aperture/shutter/light

The precise noise model can be simplified, but the educational behavior should be convincing.

⸻

ND Filter

ND should reduce incoming light by stops without directly changing:

* shutter speed
* aperture
* ISO

This could be a particularly useful teaching tool for showing why ND exists.

Possible values:

* Off
* ND 0.3 / 1 stop
* ND 0.6 / 2 stops
* ND 0.9 / 3 stops
* etc.

The specific UI terminology can be refined.

⸻

Exposure Model

I would like the simulator to use stops in a meaningful way.

The coding agent can choose the exact internal architecture, but conceptually something like:

Exposure contribution =
scene light
× aperture contribution
× shutter duration
× ND attenuation

Then ISO/gain occurs later in the imaging pipeline.

It may be useful to internally track exposure in EV / stops, even if the student does not always see those numbers.

A useful traditional relationship is:

EV100 = log2(N² / t)

where:

* N = f-number
* t = shutter time

We do not necessarily need to teach that formula directly to beginning students.

But a physically meaningful underlying model is desirable.

⸻

Color / White Balance

I want white balance to behave meaningfully rather than simply placing a yellow or blue overlay on the image.

A good middle-ground approach may be:

* internally work in linear-light RGB
* approximate illuminant color temperature
* apply white-balance correction using RGB gains or another physically motivated color transform
* gamma-encode for display afterward

I am open to better approaches.

The important thing pedagogically is that students should experience something like:

3200K lighting + incorrect 5600K camera WB → visibly warm/orange image

or the reverse:

5600K daylight + incorrect 3200K WB → visibly blue image

Then setting the correct white balance should neutralize appropriate objects.

Eventually it might be useful to support mixed lighting, where no single white balance perfectly corrects everything.

That does not necessarily need to be in the earliest implementation, but the architecture should not make it impossible.

⸻

Linear RGB vs Display RGB

One technical concern I have already thought about is that simply manipulating 0–255 RGB values is not physically correct.

A reasonable approach seems to be:

1. Convert source imagery from sRGB into linear-light RGB.
2. Perform exposure / light / color calculations in linear space.
3. Apply tone mapping / clipping / sensor-like behavior as appropriate.
4. Convert back into sRGB or another display representation.

I am open to the coding agent recommending a better implementation.

The main goal is to avoid naive RGB arithmetic if something only modestly more sophisticated would produce much more realistic behavior.

⸻

Focus and Depth of Field

I want focus and depth of field in the MVP.

The exact simulation method can be simplified.

The student should be able to understand that:

* Focus has a plane / distance.
* Objects can be in or out of focus.
* Wider aperture generally produces shallower depth of field.
* Smaller aperture generally produces greater depth of field.

If possible, focal length and sensor size could eventually affect this too.

Those might not need to be exposed as student controls immediately, but the architecture should ideally allow them later.

A scene that contains foreground, subject, and background elements would be useful for demonstrating this.

⸻

Motion Blur

Motion blur should respond meaningfully to shutter speed.

This is important because students often understand shutter only as an exposure control.

I want them to see that it also changes motion rendering.

For example:

* 1/30 → relatively smeary motion
* 1/60 → more conventional motion
* 1/250 → noticeably crisp / staccato
* 1/1000 → extremely crisp individual frames

Some visual object in the scene should move so students can actually see the change.

Possible examples:

* rotating fan
* swinging object
* moving hand
* performer
* metronome
* moving drumstick
* walking person

The implementation can decide what is practical.

⸻

Bad Settings Should Be Allowed

The simulator should absolutely allow students to make bad choices.

Do not protect them from:

* Blown highlights
* Crushed shadows
* Excessive ISO noise
* Wrong white balance
* Excessive motion blur
* Unnaturally fast shutter
* Missed focus
* Bad depth-of-field decisions
* Strange combinations of settings

That experimentation is educational.

Ideally the simulator lets the student get into trouble and then diagnose the problem.

⸻

Visual Measurement / Monitoring Modules

I would like monitoring tools to be built as modular overlays/components.

Possible modules include:

* Histogram
* Waveform monitor
* Zebras
* Highlight clipping indicators
* Exposure meter
* EV / stops readout
* Focus aids
* Depth-of-field visualization
* Noise display / magnifier
* Motion blur visualization

These should not all appear at once by default.

An activity should be able to turn specific tools on or off.

For example:

A beginner exposure activity might show:

* image
* aperture
* exposure meter

A later activity might add:

* histogram
* zebras

A challenge could hide everything except the camera controls.

⸻

Pedagogical Modes

I like the idea of having a few different modes similar to some of my other educational webapps.

The architecture should support something roughly like:

Guided Mode

This teaches the student what happens.

It can:

* explain concepts
* lock certain variables
* ask the student to change one setting
* immediately show consequences
* show stop changes
* give hints
* identify mistakes
* display overlays

For example:

“Change aperture from f/4 to f/2.8.”

Then it might show:

“+1 stop”

and explain:

“The aperture opening became larger, allowing approximately twice as much light to reach the sensor.”

Guided mode can be more explicit.

⸻

Playground

Students can freely explore.

Potentially:

* choose a scene
* manipulate camera controls
* enable/disable monitoring tools
* reset camera
* compare settings

Very little instructional interference.

⸻

Challenge / Mission Mode

This should hide some of the teaching information.

Example:

“You are filming a performer at 30 fps.

The image is too dark.

Maintain natural-looking motion.

Do not raise ISO above 800.

Adjust the camera to get an appropriate exposure.”

The app checks whether their solution falls within acceptable ranges.

There should often be multiple valid solutions.

I do not want every challenge to secretly expect one exact combination of settings.

That is important.

Real camera work involves tradeoffs.

⸻

Quiz Mode

Possibly included, but not necessarily required immediately.

Questions could include things like:

“What change would increase exposure by one stop without affecting motion blur?”

or

“You are filming at 30 fps and want conventional motion blur. Which shutter speed is most appropriate?”

But the simulator itself should remain more important than multiple-choice questions.

⸻

Guided Mode vs Challenge Mode Feedback

A distinction I especially like:

Guided mode

Can explicitly tell students things such as:

* +1 stop
* -2 stops
* shutter too slow
* highlight clipping
* white balance mismatch
* increased noise
* depth of field became shallower

Challenge mode

Should generally make students diagnose these effects themselves.

It might give feedback after they submit, rather than narrating every adjustment.

Exposure Stops as an Educational Concept

Stops are an important concept.

Students should eventually internalize relationships like:

Aperture

f/2.8 → f/4 = -1 stop

Shutter

1/60 → 1/125 ≈ -1 stop

ISO

ISO 400 → ISO 800 = +1 stop of gain

ND

ND 0 → 1-stop ND = -1 stop of incoming light

One powerful teaching feature could be showing equivalent exposure combinations.

For example:

f/4
1/60
ISO 400

could produce similar brightness to:

f/2.8
1/125
ISO 400

but look different because depth of field and motion blur changed.

That distinction is extremely valuable pedagogically.

⸻

Exposure Is Not the Only Goal

The simulator should teach that simply making the image “the right brightness” is not sufficient.

Students should consider:

* Motion rendering
* Noise
* Depth of field
* Focus
* Highlight preservation
* White balance
* Frame rate
* Creative or practical intent

A challenge might therefore reject an otherwise correctly exposed result because the student created unacceptable motion blur or extreme noise.

⸻

Monitoring / Scope Accuracy

Monitoring tools do not necessarily need to be broadcast-engineering-perfect in v1.

However, they should be internally consistent with the rendered image.

For example:

If the image clips, the waveform and histogram should reflect that clipping.

If exposure changes, the scopes should change appropriately.

This would make them genuinely useful teaching tools rather than decorative graphics.

Student Experience

Ideally a student should be able to open the app and within seconds start manipulating something.

Minimal upfront reading.

The app can teach through interaction.

For example:

Make the image brighter.

Student opens aperture.

Image changes immediately.

Then:

Great. You gained one stop of exposure. What else changed?

The student notices that the background becomes blurrier.

That sort of cause-and-effect learning is exactly what I want.

Do Not Overload Beginners

Even though the simulator itself may support many parameters, introductory lessons should isolate concepts.

Example progression:

Station 1 — Aperture

Only aperture is adjustable.

Student observes:

* exposure
* depth of field

Station 2 — Shutter

Only shutter speed is adjustable.

Student observes:

* exposure
* motion blur

Station 3 — ISO

Only ISO adjustable.

Student observes:

* brightness
* noise

Station 4 — Exposure Triangle

Aperture + shutter + ISO.

Station 5 — Frame Rate + Shutter

Teach appropriate shutter relationships.

Station 6 — White Balance

Teach color temperature.

Station 7 — ND

Teach maintaining aperture/shutter in bright light.

Station 8 — Full Camera Challenge

Everything unlocked.

This is roughly the pedagogical direction.

I want a visually responsive simulator.

Avoid long rerenders.

If high-quality effects are expensive, consider:

* better render after interaction stops
* GPU shaders
* precomputed layers
* caching

Modern phones should still feel fluid.
